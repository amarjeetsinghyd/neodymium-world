GE’s F404 Supply Slump Stalls Tejas Mk1A Production Lines

Amarjeet Singh Senior Analyst
Nov 17, 2026 7 Min Read

Strategic Intelligence Desk: Curated and verified by Senior Analyst Amarjeet Singh. Directed toward defense sovereignty, Indo-Pacific deterrence, and critical emerging technologies.

Key Takeaways

  • • GE Aerospace is expected to deliver between two and four F404-IN20 engines in November, bringing cumulative deliveries under the 2021 contract to 16-18 units.
  • • The ongoing supply shortfall disrupts HAL's final assembly lines, leaving multiple Tejas Mk1A airframes completed but grounded without propulsion systems.
  • • Meeting the government's year-end target of 22 cumulative engines now hinges entirely on an aggressive delivery surge throughout December.
  • • The broader 99-engine baseline contract and the subsequent 113-engine follow-on order for 97 additional jets face downstream scheduling vulnerabilities.

The Propulsion Bottleneck at HAL's Assembly Plants

The intricate machinery of Atmanirbhar Bharat’s aerospace ambitions faces a severe test on the factory floor as global supply chains stutter. General Electric Aerospace is slated to deliver a modest two to four F404-IN20 engines to Hindustan Aeronautics Limited over the course of November. This projected trickle of high-value hardware falls conspicuously short of the anticipated four-engines-per-month cadence promised by state planners. For HAL executives watching completed airframes roll off the line only to be parked indefinitely, the delay transforms a logistical friction point into a strategic headache.

Following a modest delivery of three units in August and a brief uptick to four units in September, cumulative handovers under the baseline 2021 contract stand at 14 engines. Optimistic projections presented at high-level defense forums had previously established a steady baseline of twelve engines slated across the final quarter. Yet, industrial reality on the ground in Lynn, Massachusetts, and within tier-one component suppliers tells a different story. If GE manages only two powerplants this month, the cumulative total crawls to 16; even a best-case scenario of four yields a modest 18 units by November 30.

The Stalled Airframe Dilemma

The ramifications of this hardware starvation extend far beyond simple delivery metrics, creating a cascading backlog across India's domestic defense ecosystem. HAL has successfully demonstrated its capacity to manufacture the composite airframes of the Tejas Mk1A at an impressive clip. However, an advanced multi-role combat aircraft without its designated powerplant is little more than an expensive static display. Without the F404-IN20 securely mounted in the fuselage, engineers cannot initiate critical post-assembly ground tests, avionics integration, or rigorous flight-line trials.

Defence Secretary Rajesh Kumar Singh had previously outlined an aggressive rebound for the final quarter to safeguard the Indian Air Force's operational readiness schedules. The reality of a two-to-three engine delivery rate for November places an immense burden on December's logistical output. To meet the government's ambitious threshold of 22 cumulative engines by year's end, extraordinary measures will be required from GE's manufacturing facilities. Every week of delay translates directly into deferred operational squadrons for an air force grappling with a dwindling fighter squadron strength.

Scaling the Domestic Manufacturing Ambition

This persistent propulsion deficit underscores the delicate balancing act defining modern Indian defense procurement. The original 2021 procurement contract called for 99 F404-IN20 engines to power the initial 83 Tejas Mk1A fighters ordered by the IAF. Recognizing both the expanding requirements of the air force and the protracted timelines of indigenous alternatives like the Kaveri engine revival, a subsequent order for 113 engines was initiated to support an additional batch of 97 aircraft. Yet, the foundational vulnerability remains tied to single-source foreign propulsion dependencies.

While the long-term vision of Atmanirbhar Bharat champions absolute self-reliance, the immediate operational reality requires managing complex transnational supply webs. GE’s struggles with tier-two supply chain bottlenecks, raw material shortages, and post-pandemic labor constraints have reverberated directly into South Asian strategic calculations. HAL’s production lines are inherently constrained by the velocity of foreign Original Equipment Manufacturers, highlighting why indigenous aero-engine design remains the ultimate holy grail for New Delhi's military-industrial complex.

The December Crunch and Strategic Fallout

As the calendar turns toward the final weeks of 2026, the spotlight shifts entirely to December's output. If HAL and the IAF are to salvage any semblance of the original delivery roadmap, GE must execute an unprecedented logistical recovery. The shortfall experienced in November compresses the window for final integration and acceptance testing, squeezing the margins for error to near zero. Every single engine shipped over the coming weeks dictates how rapidly frontline squadrons can absorb the enhanced capabilities of the Mk1A platform.

Ultimately, this episode serves as a sobering case study in defense industrial planning. It demonstrates that while structural reforms and financial outlays can rapidly accelerate domestic airframe manufacturing, critical sub-assemblies will continue to dictate the actual tempo of military power projection. Until indigenous propulsion initiatives reach maturity, Indian defense planners will remain hostage to the production realities of traditional Western aerospace giants.
