Pacific Pivot: Japan, South Korea Counter China's Naval Ascent

Amarjeet Singh Senior Analyst
Sep 10, 2026 7 Min Read

Strategic Intelligence Desk: Curated and verified by Senior Analyst Amarjeet Singh. Directed toward defense sovereignty, Indo-Pacific deterrence, and critical emerging technologies.

Key Takeaways

  • • China's rapid naval expansion, including 39 warships and 550,000 tonnes of displacement in five years, is the primary driver for regional defense modernization.
  • • Japan is investing in multi-role Aegis System Equipped Vessels (ASEV), F-35B capable Izumo-class carriers, and advanced Taigei-class submarines to bolster air-and-missile defense and power projection.
  • • South Korea is pursuing nuclear-powered attack submarines and indigenous KDDX destroyers, crucial for countering North Korea's subsurface threats and enhancing long-range maritime endurance.
  • • The modernization efforts by Japan and South Korea are critical for securing East Asia's economy, protecting global supply chains, and reinforcing the Western alliance's deterrence architecture in the Indo-Pacific.

Engineering Standoff Superiority: Japan's Maritime Reinvention

SINGAPORE – The Indo-Pacific faces a new maritime reality. China's People's Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) has unleashed an unprecedented shipbuilding surge, dwarfing regional rivals with 39 warships and 550,000 tonnes of displacement between 2019 and 2023 alone. This raw tonnage, coupled with a fleet now exceeding the U.S. Navy in hull count at 332 warships to America's 319, is forcing Washington's key allies, Japan and South Korea, into a rapid, high-stakes naval modernization race. This scramble isn't merely about matching numbers; it's a critical strategic imperative to secure the lifeblood of East Asian economies – seaborne trade – and to establish credible deterrence against an ascendant China and an increasingly belligerent, nuclear-armed North Korea, whose own submarine ambitions further complicate the security calculus.

Japan’s defense posture, long constrained by constitutional interpretations, has undergone a fundamental shift, with record defense budgets allocated annually over the past decade. Central to this transformation is the Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force (JMSDF)’s acquisition of two Aegis System Equipped Vessels (ASEV). These 14,000-ton platforms, slated for commissioning in 2028 and 2029, represent a decisive step in bolstering Japan’s ballistic missile defense (BMD) capabilities, integrating seamlessly with its existing Aegis-equipped destroyers. Each ASEV will feature the formidable Lockheed-Martin SPY-7(V)1 multi-function radar and a substantial 128 Mk.41 Vertical Launch System (VLS) cells, capable of deploying SM-2, SM-3, and SM-6 air-and-missile defense interceptors, alongside Type 12 anti-ship missiles and Tomahawk cruise missiles, projecting a multi-layered defense shield across its airspace and critical sea lanes.

Beyond BMD, Japan is rapidly enhancing its power projection and anti-submarine warfare capabilities. The conversion of two Izumo-class Helicopter-Destroyers into full-fledged aircraft carriers for the Lockheed-Martin F-35B Short Take-off and Vertical Landing (STOVL) stealth fighter is a game-changer. The Izumo's conversion, expected to conclude by early 2027, and the Kaga by fiscal 2027, will provide the JMSDF with organic fixed-wing air power, significantly extending its operational reach and enabling forward deterrence in the East China Sea and beyond. Concurrently, Japan is systematically replacing its older Oyashio-class submarines with the advanced Taigei-class, commissioning one new boat annually since 2022, ensuring a potent, stealthy subsurface deterrent against growing regional naval activity. Further augmenting its fleet, Japan continues the build of the Mogami-class frigate, with an improved design already chosen by Australia and under consideration for New Zealand’s frigate program, alongside the introduction of Sakura-class Offshore Patrol Vessels and unmanned transport vessels for its outlying islands, directly addressing territorial defense in the East China Sea.

Seoul's Subsurface Gambit: Nuclear Power and Indigenous Prowess

South Korea, a shipbuilding titan, is leveraging its robust industrial base to forge a formidable naval force, increasingly through indigenous systems. A pivotal development is the recent agreement with the U.S. to allow South Korea to develop its own nuclear-powered submarines. This move is a direct response to North Korea's accelerating nuclear submarine program, which seeks to deploy submarine-launched ballistic missiles. South Korean nuclear-powered attack submarines will offer significantly greater endurance than their diesel-electric counterparts, enabling extended patrols to track North Korean ballistic missile submarines and to rapidly respond to land-based missile launches, as highlighted by defense analysts. This capability fundamentally alters the subsurface deterrence calculus on the Korean Peninsula.

Concurrently, the Republic of Korea Navy (ROKN) is advancing its ambitious KDDX destroyer program. This new class of 7,000-ton destroyers is designed to be equipped predominantly with indigenous systems, including its integrated sensor suite, combat management system, VLS, and missiles, though propulsion will rely on the proven GE Aerospace LM2500+G4 marine gas turbine engine. Hanwha Ocean, tasked with the design and construction of the first KDDX due for service in 2032, exemplifies South Korea’s commitment to self-reliance in cutting-edge naval technology. This blend of indigenous innovation and strategic partnerships underscores Seoul's determination to maintain a qualitative edge in a rapidly evolving threat landscape, as further evidenced by Hanwha's recent win for a Thai frigate contract against international rivals.

"The race to modernize isn't just about new ships; it's about redefining the very architecture of deterrence in a region where economic lifelines are inseparable from strategic power and where the margin for error is shrinking."

The Contested Chokepoints: Deterrence in a New Maritime Order

The accelerated naval modernization by Japan and South Korea is not merely a defensive reaction; it represents a proactive reshaping of deterrence in the Indo-Pacific. The strategic importance of protecting vital sea lanes, through which an estimated 90% of global trade transits, cannot be overstated. For East Asian economies, deeply reliant on seaborne trade, a bolstered navy is an existential necessity. The deployment of advanced BMD platforms, carrier-capable stealth fighters, and long-endurance submarines directly addresses the challenges posed by China's anti-access/area denial (A2/AD) capabilities and North Korea's asymmetric threats, ensuring crucial chokepoints remain open for commerce and security.

These investments reinforce the U.S.-led alliance system, providing interoperable, high-end assets that complicate potential aggressor calculus. Japan's Mogami-class frigates, with an improved design already adopted by Australia and under consideration by New Zealand, illustrate a growing regional collaboration that extends beyond bilateral U.S. partnerships. Furthermore, Japan's development of Sakura-class Offshore Patrol Vessels and unmanned transport vessels for outlying islands directly addresses territorial defense in the East China Sea, a critical flashpoint where China's maritime claims are increasingly assertive. This multi-faceted approach aims to preserve the fragile balance of power and ensure freedom of navigation in contested waters, vital for global stability and critical supply chain hegemony.

Beyond the Hull: Doctrine, Integration, and Supply Chain Resilience

The efficacy of these new platforms hinges not just on their individual capabilities, but on their seamless integration into evolving doctrines and command-and-control structures. For both Japan and South Korea, interoperability with U.S. forces remains paramount, ensuring a cohesive response to regional contingencies. This requires continuous joint exercises, shared intelligence, and standardized communication protocols to maximize the deterrent effect and operational readiness of the allied fleets. The strategic value of these modernizations extends beyond raw military power, contributing to the broader goal of critical supply chain hegemony by safeguarding maritime trade routes.

Moreover, the drive towards indigenization, particularly evident in South Korea's KDDX program, reflects a deeper strategic imperative: to reduce reliance on foreign suppliers and enhance national resilience in critical defense technologies. While platforms like the ASEVs still rely on key U.S. components such as the SPY-7 radar and Tomahawk missiles, the trend towards domestic production strengthens national defense industrial bases. This balance between acquiring cutting-edge foreign technology and cultivating indigenous capabilities is crucial for long-term strategic autonomy, ensuring that these nations can sustain their advanced fleets and adapt to future threats without undue external dependencies. The current wave of naval investment is therefore not merely a procurement cycle, but a foundational shift in how these nations perceive and project their security in a volatile Indo-Pacific.
