<h2>Historical Context of India's Computing Ambitions</h2><p>I analyze how India's post-independence computing initiatives faltered despite initial promise. The Tata Institute of Fundamental Research developed TIFRAC around 1960, demonstrating indigenous capability. Yet the state failed to cultivate a broader computing industry around this achievement.</p><h2>Strategic Failures in Policy Implementation</h2><p>IBM dominated the Indian market with outdated machines until 1977, when Fernandes expelled them. This created a vacuum that India couldn't fill despite ECIL's development of the TDC-12 semiconductor-based computer. The government's attempts to build component manufacturing capacity were undermined by inter-departmental politics and Cold War constraints.</p><h2>Critical Hardware-Software Disconnect</h2><p>I project that India's decision to separate hardware development from software services proved catastrophic. This choice created a dependency trap that persists in today's semiconductor challenges. The technocratic elite mistook technical services for genuine technological independence.</p><h2>Expert Commentary</h2><p>Banerjee's analysis reveals how colonial structures shaped India's technological trajectory. Yet I argue the book underestimates India's agency in making reversible policy choices. The structural determinism framework obscures critical decision points where different paths were possible.</p><h2>Strategic Forecasting</h2><p>I forecast that India's current semiconductor push will fail unless it addresses the fundamental hardware-software integration mistake. The concentration of fabrication and IP in Taiwan and the US mirrors past dependency patterns. Without strategic industrial policy, India risks becoming a mere assembly hub rather than a design powerhouse.</p><h2>Contemporary Implications</h2><p>The renewable energy supply chain extractive dynamics represent a new form of technological colonialism. India's semiconductor strategy must prioritize integrated development rather than component-level self-sufficiency. The revolutionary promise of decolonization requires concrete industrial policy, not rhetorical transformation.</p>
